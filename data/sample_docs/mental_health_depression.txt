DEPRESSION AND MOOD DISORDERS

Source: Educational content based on WHO, NHS, and APA (American Psychiatric Association) DSM-5 guidelines

OVERVIEW
Depressive disorders are among the most prevalent mental health conditions globally, affecting an estimated 280 million people worldwide. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is more than transient sadness or temporary grief; it is a serious medical illness characterized by persistent emotional, cognitive, somatic, and behavioral disruptions that interfere significantly with daily functioning, work, and personal relationships.

DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA (DSM-5 / ICD-11 Standards)
A diagnosis of Major Depressive Disorder requires the presence of at least 5 of the following 9 symptoms nearly every day for a minimum of two consecutive weeks, with at least one symptom being either depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure:
1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day (feeling sad, empty, hopeless, or tearful)
2. Marked diminished interest or pleasure (anhedonia) in all, or almost all, activities previously enjoyed
3. Significant unintentional weight loss when not dieting, weight gain, or decrease/increase in appetite
4. Insomnia (difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or early morning awakening) or hypersomnia (excessive daytime sleeping)
5. Psychomotor agitation (restlessness, pacing) or psychomotor retardation (slowed speech and movements)
6. Fatigue or loss of energy nearly every day
7. Feelings of worthlessness or excessive, inappropriate guilt
8. Diminished ability to think, concentrate, or make decisions
9. Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation without a specific plan, suicide attempt, or a specific plan for committing suicide

SUBTYPES OF DEPRESSIVE DISORDERS
- Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia): Chronic depressed mood lasting for at least 2 years in adults, typically less severe than MDD but enduring.
- Perinatal / Postpartum Depression: Severe depressive episodes occurring during pregnancy or within the weeks/months following childbirth.
- Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD): Depressive episodes exhibiting a seasonal pattern, typically recurring in late autumn and winter months, associated with reduced daylight exposure.
- Bipolar Depression: Depressive episodes occurring as part of Bipolar I or II disorder, alternating with manic or hypomanic episodes. Treating bipolar depression with standard antidepressants alone requires extreme caution due to the risk of inducing manic switching.

CLINICAL ASSESSMENT AND SCREENING
Standardized validated clinical rating scales used to screen and monitor symptom severity include:
- PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9): 9-item self-report questionnaire scoring severity from 0 to 27 (scores 10-14 indicate moderate, 15-19 moderately severe, 20-27 severe depression). Item 9 specifically assesses suicidal thoughts.
- GAD-7: Often co-administered given high comorbidity with anxiety disorders.

EVIDENCE-BASED TREATMENT APPROACHES

1. Psychotherapy:
- Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): The most thoroughly researched psychological intervention. Helps patients identify and restructure negative cognitive distortions, maladaptive automatic thoughts, and implement behavioral activation to re-engage in meaningful activities.
- Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT): Focuses on resolving interpersonal disputes, role transitions, and grief.
- Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT): Particularly effective in preventing depressive relapse in individuals with recurrent depression.

2. Pharmacotherapy:
Indicated particularly for moderate to severe depressive episodes:
- Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs): First-line agents (e.g., sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, citalopram). Generally favorable tolerability and safety profile. Common initial side effects: mild nausea, headache, transient sleep disruption, and sexual dysfunction.
- Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs): E.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine. Useful when depression is accompanied by neuropathic or chronic pain.
- Atypical Antidepressants: E.g., bupropion (norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor, minimal sexual side effects), mirtazapine (sedating, promotes sleep and appetite).
- Crucial Clinical Considerations: Antidepressant medications typically require 2 to 4 weeks (sometimes up to 6 weeks) before therapeutic benefits become evident. Treatment should be continued for at least 6 to 9 months following symptom remission to prevent relapse. Discontinuation should occur via gradual tapering under medical supervision to avoid antidepressant discontinuation syndrome.

3. Lifestyle Interventions:
- Regular aerobic exercise (150 minutes/week) stimulates neurogenesis and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF).
- Healthy dietary habits (Mediterranean pattern) and adequate sleep hygiene.
- Social connectedness and peer support groups.

CRISIS SUPPORT AND SUICIDE PREVENTION
If you or someone you know is experiencing acute suicidal thoughts, overwhelming distress, or self-harm impulses:
- In the UK: Call 111 (NHS Mental Health) or Call Samaritans on 116 123 (free 24/7).
- In the US/Canada: Call or Text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, free 24/7).
- In emergencies or immediate physical danger: Call 999 or 911 or go directly to the nearest hospital emergency department.
