SLEEP HEALTH AND SLEEP DISORDERS

Source: Educational content based on American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and NHS guidelines

OVERVIEW
Sleep is an active, essential biological process necessary for physiological restoration, memory consolidation, immune regulation, metabolic homeostasis, and cardiovascular health. Healthy sleep is determined by adequate duration, good quality, appropriate timing, and the absence of sleep disorders. Adults typically require 7 to 9 hours of restorative sleep each night for optimal functioning and long-term health.

SLEEP DURATION RECOMMENDATIONS ACROSS THE LIFESPAN:
- Newborns (0-3 months): 14 to 17 hours daily
- Infants (4-11 months): 12 to 15 hours daily
- Toddlers (1-2 years): 11 to 14 hours daily
- Preschoolers (3-5 years): 10 to 13 hours daily
- School-age Children (6-12 years): 9 to 12 hours daily
- Teenagers (13-18 years): 8 to 10 hours daily
- Adults (18-64 years): 7 to 9 hours daily
- Older Adults (65+ years): 7 to 8 hours daily

SLEEP ARCHITECTURE AND STAGES
Sleep cycles repeat approximately every 90 to 110 minutes, alternating between two main phases:
1. Non-Rapid Eye Movement (NREM) Sleep:
   - Stage N1: Light sleep transition between wakefulness and sleep.
   - Stage N2: Deeper baseline sleep with sleep spindles and K-complexes; heart rate and body temperature decrease.
   - Stage N3 (Slow-Wave / Deep Sleep): Restorative delta sleep during which growth hormone is secreted, tissue repair occurs, cellular waste products are cleared via the glymphatic system, and physical recovery takes place.
2. Rapid Eye Movement (REM) Sleep: Characterized by rapid conjugate eye movements, muscle atonia (paralysis), dreaming, and heightened brain activity. Essential for emotional processing, procedural memory, and cognitive integration.

CONSEQUENCES OF CHRONIC SLEEP DEPRIVATION
Insufficient or fragmented sleep significantly increases the risk of:
- Cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, and stroke
- Metabolic disruption, including obesity, impaired glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes
- Weakened immune response, leading to increased susceptibility to viral and bacterial infections
- Neurocognitive impairment, including slowed reaction times, reduced executive function, poor decision-making, and increased risk of motor vehicle accidents
- Psychiatric conditions, such as major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety, and emotional dysregulation

COMMON SLEEP DISORDERS

1. Insomnia:
Characterized by persistent difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening with inability to return to sleep, accompanied by daytime impairment, occurring at least 3 nights per week for at least 3 months (chronic insomnia).
- First-Line Treatment: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the gold-standard treatment, outperforming medications in long-term efficacy. Components include sleep restriction therapy, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, and relaxation training.
- Sleep Hygiene Principles:
  * Maintain a consistent sleep-wake schedule every day, including weekends.
  * Keep the bedroom dark, quiet, and cool (approximately 18 to 20 degrees Celsius / 65 to 68 degrees Fahrenheit).
  * Avoid electronic screens (smartphones, tablets, computers, television) for at least 60 minutes before bedtime due to blue light suppressing melatonin secretion.
  * Avoid caffeinated beverages within 6 to 8 hours of bedtime.
  * Avoid alcohol as a sleep aid; while sedating initially, alcohol fragments sleep architecture, reduces REM sleep, and exacerbates sleep apnea.
  * Reserve the bed only for sleep and intimacy (stimulus control).
  * Avoid vigorous exercise within 2 to 3 hours of bedtime.

2. Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA):
Characterized by repetitive episodes of upper airway collapse during sleep, resulting in oxygen desaturation, surges in sympathetic activity, and frequent micro-arousals.
- Symptoms: Loud habitual snoring, witnessed gasping or choking episodes during sleep, morning headaches, unrefreshing sleep, dry mouth on awakening, and excessive daytime sleepiness.
- Risk Factors: Obesity (BMI > 30), thick neck circumference (> 43 cm in men, > 40 cm in women), male sex, older age, craniofacial abnormalities, and smoking.
- Diagnosis: Diagnostic overnight sleep study (polysomnography) measuring the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI).
- Treatment: Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) is the gold-standard therapy, maintaining upper airway patency during sleep. Weight reduction, positional therapy, mandibular advancement splints, and avoidance of sedative medications are complementary.

3. Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS):
An uncomfortable urge to move the legs, typically accompanied by unpleasant sensations, occurring predominantly during periods of rest or inactivity in the evening, relieved temporarily by movement. Often linked to central dopaminergic dysfunction and iron deficiency; serum ferritin should be tested.

RED FLAGS AND WHEN TO SEE A DOCTOR
- Witnessed cessation of breathing or gasping during sleep
- Severe daytime sleepiness leading to falling asleep while driving or working
- Sudden attacks of muscle weakness triggered by emotion (cataplexy, a sign of narcolepsy)
- Violent dream enactment behaviors during sleep (REM sleep behavior disorder)
