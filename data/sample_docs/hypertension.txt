HYPERTENSION (HIGH BLOOD PRESSURE) – A COMPREHENSIVE OVERVIEW

Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is a chronic medical condition in which the blood pressure in the arteries is persistently elevated. Blood pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) and is expressed as two numbers: systolic pressure (the higher number, representing pressure when the heart beats) and diastolic pressure (the lower number, representing pressure when the heart rests between beats). A normal blood pressure reading is below 120/80 mmHg. Hypertension is diagnosed when readings consistently exceed 130/80 mmHg.

CLASSIFICATION
- Normal: Systolic less than 120 mmHg and diastolic less than 80 mmHg
- Elevated: Systolic 120–129 mmHg and diastolic less than 80 mmHg
- Stage 1 Hypertension: Systolic 130–139 mmHg or diastolic 80–89 mmHg
- Stage 2 Hypertension: Systolic 140 mmHg or higher, or diastolic 90 mmHg or higher
- Hypertensive Crisis: Systolic above 180 mmHg and/or diastolic above 120 mmHg

CAUSES AND RISK FACTORS
Primary (essential) hypertension accounts for approximately 95% of cases and has no identifiable cause. Secondary hypertension results from underlying conditions such as kidney disease, adrenal gland tumours, or thyroid disorders.

Risk factors include:
- Advanced age (risk increases significantly after age 65)
- Family history of hypertension
- Obesity and overweight status
- Sedentary lifestyle and physical inactivity
- High sodium (salt) intake
- Low potassium, calcium, or magnesium intake
- Excessive alcohol consumption
- Tobacco smoking
- Chronic stress and psychological disorders
- Sleep apnea and other sleep disorders
- Diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome
- Chronic kidney disease
- Certain medications (NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, decongestants, stimulants)

SYMPTOMS
Hypertension is often called the "silent killer" because most people with high blood pressure experience no symptoms, even when readings are dangerously elevated. When symptoms do occur, they may include:
- Severe headaches, particularly at the back of the head (occipital headaches)
- Nosebleeds (epistaxis)
- Shortness of breath
- Dizziness and lightheadedness
- Visual changes or blurred vision
- Chest pain or pressure
- Irregular heartbeat (palpitations)
- Blood in urine (haematuria)
- Facial flushing

Hypertensive crisis (blood pressure above 180/120 mmHg) is a medical emergency that can cause stroke, heart attack, heart failure, or kidney damage.

DIAGNOSIS
Blood pressure should be measured at least twice on two separate occasions. Home blood pressure monitoring and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) provide additional information to confirm the diagnosis and assess white-coat hypertension.

COMPLICATIONS
Untreated hypertension can lead to:
- Stroke (haemorrhagic or ischaemic)
- Coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction (heart attack)
- Heart failure (left ventricular hypertrophy progressing to dysfunction)
- Aortic aneurysm and dissection
- Chronic kidney disease and renal failure
- Peripheral arterial disease
- Retinopathy and vision loss
- Cognitive impairment and vascular dementia
- Sexual dysfunction

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT

Lifestyle Modifications (first-line for Stage 1):
- Weight reduction: Even a 5–10 kg weight loss can reduce systolic blood pressure by 5–20 mmHg
- DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension): Rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy; limits saturated fat and sodium
- Sodium restriction: Less than 2,300 mg/day (ideally under 1,500 mg/day)
- Regular aerobic exercise: At least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week
- Alcohol moderation: No more than 2 drinks per day for men, 1 for women
- Smoking cessation
- Stress management: Meditation, yoga, deep breathing exercises

Medications:
- ACE inhibitors (e.g., Lisinopril, Enalapril, Ramipril): Block angiotensin-converting enzyme, reducing vasoconstriction. Common side effect: dry persistent cough.
- Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers – ARBs (e.g., Losartan, Valsartan, Olmesartan): Block the action of angiotensin II. Better tolerated than ACE inhibitors.
- Calcium Channel Blockers (e.g., Amlodipine, Nifedipine, Diltiazem): Relax blood vessels and reduce heart rate.
- Thiazide Diuretics (e.g., Hydrochlorothiazide, Chlorthalidone): Reduce fluid volume. First-choice for uncomplicated hypertension.
- Beta-Blockers (e.g., Metoprolol, Atenolol, Carvedilol): Reduce heart rate and cardiac output. Particularly useful with concurrent heart disease.
- Aldosterone Antagonists (e.g., Spironolactone): Useful in resistant hypertension.
- Alpha-Blockers (e.g., Doxazosin): Used as add-on therapy.
- Central agonists (e.g., Clonidine, Methyldopa): Used in pregnancy-related hypertension.

Resistant hypertension (failure to control with three or more agents) may require specialist referral and additional medications or procedures such as renal denervation.

MONITORING
Patients should monitor blood pressure at home, attend regular follow-up appointments, and have periodic blood tests (electrolytes, renal function, lipid profile) and urine tests for protein.
